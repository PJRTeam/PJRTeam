Everyone on Your Payroll Is Carrying a Broadcast Station

Ten years ago this month, CMS told every nursing home in America to build a policy for it. Most still haven’t built a system.

From PJR Team — for skilled nursing and senior living operators

On August 5, 2016, CMS issued Survey & Certification memo 16-33-NH to state survey agency directors. The subject line was blunt: protecting resident privacy and prohibiting mental abuse related to photographs and audio/video recordings by nursing home staff.

The memo did three things that still govern how your facility gets surveyed today. It established that taking an unauthorized photo or recording of a resident—in any state of dress or undress, using any device—violates that resident’s right to privacy and confidentiality. It established that a photo or recording does not have to be posted anywhere to constitute mental abuse. And it directed surveyors to request your social media and abuse-prohibition policies on site, to evaluate whether you actually have one.

Note the third point. The exposure is not only the incident. It is the absence of the system that was supposed to prevent it.

A decade later, the incidents haven’t stopped

In October 2025, a report funded by the Colorado State Long-Term Care Ombudsman Program and compiled by gerontologist Eilon Caspi examined 100 separate incidents in which staff photographed or recorded residents and posted the material to social platforms. The incidents spanned 30 states and occurred between March 2017 and April 2025—meaning every one of them happened after CMS told the industry to fix it.

The details operators should sit with:

  • 147 residents were depicted across those 100 incidents.
  • 132 staff members were primary perpetrators. Another 22 employees received, shared, or otherwise interacted with the content and said nothing.
  • Roughly three-quarters (73%) of primary perpetrators were CNAs or nursing assistants. The remaining 27% ranged from housekeeping to admissions.
  • About 85% of the victims had some level of cognitive impairment.

That second bullet is the one that should reshape your training program. For every perpetrator, there was roughly one additional employee who saw it and didn’t report it. That is not a bad-apple problem. That is a culture-and-training problem, and it is squarely within an administrator’s control.

Caspi also documented something harder to hear: in some incidents, administrators and managers minimized what had happened. In those cases, no internal investigation was opened and no report was filed with the state survey agency—which, in his assessment, may have allowed the conduct to continue.

Why training alone decays

Here is the structural reason this keeps happening in skilled nursing specifically, and it isn’t about character.

A landmark Health Affairs analysis of payroll-based journal data across 15,645 facilities found median annual nursing staff turnover of roughly 94%, with a mean of about 128%. Subsequent research found that in an average week, roughly 15% of nursing hours are delivered by employees hired within the previous 90 days.

Run that against an annual in-service. If you train on device use, resident consent, and social media conduct once a year, a meaningful share of the people on your floor at any given moment were not in the room when you did it. The policy exists. The knowledge doesn’t.

And CMS anticipated this. The guidance is explicit that providing in-service education does not, by itself, relieve the facility of responsibility for implementation. You are accountable for oversight and supervision, not for having held a meeting.

The second exposure: what’s in the background

Most facilities that get this wrong don’t get it wrong through malice. They get it wrong through a marketing photo.

An activities coordinator captures a genuinely lovely moment at a birthday party. It goes up on Facebook that afternoon. In the frame: a census board, a resident wristband, a med cart with a chart open, an EHR screen, a room-number placard next to an identifiable face, or a resident in the background whose family never signed a release.

That is protected health information disclosed to the public, and it is now indexed, cached, and screenshot.

HIPAA civil monetary penalties adjusted upward effective January 28, 2026. The current tiers run from $145 per violation at the low end to $73,011 per violation, with the top tier—willful neglect, not timely corrected—reaching $2,190,294. “We didn’t know” is a tier, not a defense. And the tier that applies to you is determined substantially by whether you had a program, documented it, and enforced it.

The third exposure: who holds the keys

Take the turnover figures above and apply them to credentials rather than clinical care.

Across the average operator, the people who have historically held the logins to the facility’s Google Business Profile, Facebook page, Instagram account, review platforms, and website CMS are marketing coordinators, activities directors, EDs, and—often—an agency or a former employee’s personal email address. Those roles turn over. The credentials frequently do not.

The failure mode is predictable, and we have watched it play out repeatedly: someone leaves, and the facility’s public identity leaves with them. Nobody can post. Nobody can respond to a review. Nobody can correct wrong hours, a wrong phone number, or a wrong address. In the worst version, a departed employee still has publish rights to a page carrying your licensed name.

The internet does not have an exit interview. Content published under your facility’s name is permanent, and access you cannot revoke is a liability you cannot quantify.

Meanwhile, families have already decided

While the compliance risk accumulates quietly, the census risk accumulates in plain sight.

In U.S. News data drawn from senior living residents, 89% reported reading reviews for all (55%) or most (34%) of the communities they considered. Your Google Business Profile—the photos, the hours, the star average, and specifically whether and how you respond to complaints—is doing the work your tour used to do, before anyone calls you.

You will get negative reviews. Every facility does, including excellent ones. A one-star review from a grieving family member is not a quality signal, and everyone in this industry knows it. But an unanswered one-star review is a signal, and the signal it sends is that nobody is home.

The response is the product. A calm, HIPAA-safe reply—one that never confirms whether the person was a resident, never discusses care, and moves the conversation to a phone number—tells the next twelve families reading it exactly how your building handles conflict.

What a system actually looks like

None of this needs to be complicated. It needs to be owned. At minimum:

  • A credential inventory. Every platform carrying your facility’s name, every account with publish rights, every person who holds them—in a document your ED and corporate office can both see.
  • Corporate-level ownership of the profile. Facility staff get delegated access. The organization owns the asset. Access is provisioned on hire and revoked on separation—the same day, on the same checklist as the badge and the narcotics key.
  • Consent and release forms that are current, specific, and on file—covering photo, video, and social use, with a documented process for revocation and for residents who cannot consent.
  • A pre-publication background check. A named second set of eyes looks at every photo for PHI in the frame before it goes up. Thirty seconds, every time.
  • Device and conduct training at onboarding, not just annually, with a quarterly refresh—because your workforce is not the same workforce it was in January.
  • A documented duty to report. Twenty-two people in that report saw content and stayed quiet. Your staff need to know that seeing it and not reporting it is itself a violation.
  • A review response protocol written with your compliance officer and reviewed by your legal team, so the person responding at 9 p.m. on a Saturday isn’t improvising.

Build it with your compliance team. Build it with your in-house counsel. Build it so it survives the departure of whoever built it.
