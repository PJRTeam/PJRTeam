Who Holds the Keys to Your Front Door? Why Social Media Training Is Non-Negotiable in Healthcare

Your online presence is the front door to your facility.

From PJR Team — September blog for skilled nursing and senior living operators

Your online presence is the front door to your facility. Before a family ever tours your building, before a hospital discharge planner sends you a referral, before a prospective hire submits an application—they’ve already met you online. In fact, 84% of patients say they check online reviews before choosing a new healthcare provider, and 40% have stopped a care plan mid-course because of what they read (rater8, 2025).

So here’s the question every administrator and executive director should be asking: who holds the keys to that front door—and have they actually been trained?

Healthcare Doesn’t Get a Pass on Mistakes

Healthcare is one of the most heavily regulated and litigated industries in the country, and that reality follows you online. A post that would be a minor embarrassment for a restaurant can be a reportable violation for a skilled nursing facility.

The stakes are real and measurable:

  • Under HIPAA’s 2026 inflation-adjusted penalty structure, a single violation can cost up to $73,011—with annual caps reaching $2.19 million per violation category.
  • It doesn’t take a rogue employee to get there. One dental practice was fined $10,000 by the Office for Civil Rights simply for how it replied to patient reviews on Yelp—the response disclosed protected health information. The team member thought they were doing reputation management. They were creating a federal violation.
  • Beyond fines, social media missteps in healthcare routinely end in terminations, state board investigations, and lawsuits—and every one of those becomes its own news story attached to your name.

That’s the uncomfortable truth about digital presence in healthcare: the same platforms that fill your census can empty it.

The Problem Isn’t Social Media. It’s Untrained Hands on the Keyboard.

Most social media problems in healthcare facilities don’t come from bad people—they come from good people who were never trained. The activities director posting photos from the fall festival. The well-meaning staff member replying to a negative Google review. The nurse who shares a “day in the life” video without realizing a med cart or census board is visible in the background.

None of them set out to create risk. All of them can—in a single post.

A trained team knows:

  • What can and cannot go online—photo consent, PHI in backgrounds, resident dignity, and the gray areas in between
  • How to respond to reviews without violating HIPAA—including the counterintuitive rule that you can’t even confirm someone was a patient or resident
  • How to handle a situation when it arises—a viral complaint, a family dispute playing out in your comments, an employee post that crosses the line
  • Who owns what—a clear chain of approval so content never goes live on a guess
