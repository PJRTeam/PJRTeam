First Who, Then What: Who’s Really Running Your Community’s Digital Front Door?

Who’s standing in your digital front door?

From PJR Team — digital governance for skilled nursing and senior living operators

Jim Collins spent five years studying why some companies leap from good to great while comparable peers stall. The finding that surprised his research team most wasn’t about strategy or markets — it was about people. The great companies, Collins wrote, first got “the right people on the bus, the wrong people off the bus, and the right people in the right seats — and then they figured out where to drive it.” First who, then what.

In senior living, we obsess over the “what.” The renovated dining room, the new memory-care wing, the activities calendar, the survey scores. Those matter. But there’s a “who” question most communities have never seriously asked — and it’s quietly costing them tours: who, exactly, is running your digital front door?

The front door has moved online

Ten years ago, an adult daughter researching care for her mother started with a phone call or a drive-by. Today she starts with a search bar. Before she ever fills out a contact form, she has read your Google reviews, scrolled your Facebook page, judged the lighting in your photos, and noticed whether your last post was three days ago or three months ago. That is the front door now — and she is standing on your porch before you know she exists.

So “who is managing our social media?” is not an IT question or a marketing afterthought. It is the same question Collins asked: is the right person in that seat?

The uncomfortable audit

Here is the part most operators don’t want to hear. In a lot of communities, the honest answer to “who runs our social media?” is one of these: the activities director who is wonderful with residents and posts when she remembers; a regional marketer juggling six buildings who treats your page as the seventh priority; a vendor no one has spoken to in a year; or — genuinely — nobody is sure, because the login lives on a sticky note or in the head of someone who left in 2023.

If any of those made you wince, you have a “who” problem. It shows up in three ways:

  • Access risk. Do you know every person and app with the password to your accounts? A former employee, an ex-vendor, a lost personal phone — each is a door left unlocked. In a HIPAA-sensitive industry, an account posting on your behalf with no clear owner is not a small thing.
  • Voice risk. When no one owns the page, no one answers the review. A worried family member leaves a one-star comment at 9pm and it sits, unanswered, as the first thing your next prospect reads.
  • Momentum risk. Great communities post with intention — residents living well, staff who stay for years, real moments families recognize. Unowned pages post sporadically or not at all, and silence reads as decline.

First who

Collins’s insight is that you don’t fix this by writing a better content calendar. You fix it by putting the right who in the seat first: one clearly accountable owner for your digital presence — internal or partnered — who holds the access, protects the voice, and drives the momentum. Then, and only then, does “what should we post?” become a productive question.

What to actually do

  • Find every door. Inventory every account — Google Business Profile, Facebook, Instagram, review sites — and every person or app that can log in. Write it down.
  • Change the locks. Reset passwords, remove anyone who shouldn’t have access, and move accounts under an organizational owner, not a personal one.
  • Name the owner. Decide who is accountable for your digital front door. If no internal person has the time or skill to do it well, that isn’t a failure — it’s exactly the seat a partner should fill.
  • Set the standard. A weekly posting rhythm, a review-response window of under 24 hours, and photo practices that respect resident privacy and consent.
  • Then decide where to drive. Now the strategy conversation — the “what” — finally pays off.

Most communities can do this better, and the ones that do it first will win the tour before the family ever picks up the phone. The digital front door is already open. The only question Collins would ask is whether the right person is standing in it.
