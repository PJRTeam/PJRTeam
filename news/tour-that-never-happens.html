The Tour That Never Happens

Families decide on a phone—before admissions ever knows they exist.

By Brittney Davis, Founder of PJR Team — published in McKnight’s Long-Term Care News

This column was published in McKnight’s Long-Term Care News on August 19, 2026. Read the original on McKnight’s ↗

A daughter choosing a skilled nursing facility for her mother rarely gets four tours. Often, she gets a phone call from a hospital discharge planner, a short list of facility names, and less than a day to decide. So she does what every consumer under pressure does: she searches.

She reads the reviews, studies the photos, checks whether anyone bothered to answer the angry post from last spring, and quietly eliminates most of the list before your admissions team ever knows she exists.

The families your team never hears from are not undecided. They decided against you on a phone, based on what your facility looks like online—on Google and on your own website.

The power of reviews

Operators accept this dynamic in every other consumer category. Nobody books a hotel with a 3.1-star rating and photos from 2016. Long-term care is no exception. When the public record and the review page tell different stories, families believe the reviews—because reviews are written in the language they actually speak.

Skilled nursing also carries a burden its neighbors in assisted living do not: your quality data is already public. Care Compare puts star ratings, staffing levels, and survey history a click away. Reviews are not a vanity metric. They are a census lever.

So why is this still the most neglected asset in the building? The root cause is not laziness. It is ownership. Annual nursing staff turnover in skilled nursing routinely exceeds 50%. The person who created the Facebook page left two years ago. The Google listing is claimed by an agency nobody remembers hiring, or by nobody at all. In portfolio audits we run, it is common to find that no current employee holds the login to the single page most families will ever see.

How do you look online?

If you lead a facility or a portfolio, three questions answerable in 30 minutes will tell you where you stand.

  • 1. When did someone last respond to a review on your Google, Yelp, or Facebook page—and who, by name, owns the next one? Not “doesn’t marketing handle that?” A name.
  • 2. Would a family visiting tomorrow recognize your building and your care from your photos? Pull up your website and Google listing and compare them honestly with what a visitor will see.
  • 3. Can your team post something compelling without posting something they shouldn’t? In skilled nursing, you are a HIPAA-covered entity. The freeze most facilities feel is a training problem, not a legal inevitability. Privacy obligations are manageable with a consent-based photo program, clear rules about what never gets posted, and a short approval chain.

Facilities that train for this publish warm, specific, compliant content every week. Facilities that don’t are represented online by whatever strangers say about them.

None of this requires a big budget. It requires a decision that these platforms are operations, not decoration; an owner with a name; a quarterly 30-minute audit against the three questions above; and training so the people closest to the work can post without creating risk.

The 2024 Genworth/CareScout Cost of Care Survey put the national median cost of a private nursing home room at $127,750 a year—$111,325 for a semi-private room. That is what a single private-pay admission is worth. Census is won at the margins, and the margin now lives on a search results page.

The tour still matters when there is time for one. But you have to be on the shortlist to get it, and the shortlist is written online, on a phone, often in a single evening.
